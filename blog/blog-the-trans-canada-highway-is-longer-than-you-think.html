The Trans-Canada Highway Is Longer Than You Think (And You Need a Ferry to Finish It)

It's the one road that's supposed to tie the country together, over 7,800 kilometres from the Pacific to the Atlantic. But it doesn't behave the way a national highway should: you can't drive all of it, it skips every territory, and the only way to truly finish the route is to put your car on a boat. Here's the strange geography of Canada's most famous ribbon of asphalt.

Every country with a coast-to-coast ambition eventually builds itself a road to prove it. The United States has Route 66 and Interstate 90; Australia has the Stuart Highway. Canada has the Trans-Canada Highway, a route so long, so theoretically simple, and so geographically odd that almost everything you assume about it turns out to be slightly wrong. It is not, for instance, a single road. It is not drivable in its entirety. And the moment you think you've got it figured out, it asks you to drive your car onto a ferry.

How Long, Exactly?

The headline number you'll see is about 7,821 kilometres, a distance so large it's easier to feel than to picture. That's roughly the straight-line distance from Madrid to Moscow, give or take, except that none of it is straight. The highway starts in Victoria, British Columbia, on the Pacific, and ends in St. John's, Newfoundland, on the Atlantic. Between them sits, well, Canada.

That single figure is also a polite fiction, because the Trans-Canada Highway is not one road but a network of designated routes. Depending on which branch you take, and there are several, particularly through the Maritimes, you can clock different mileages. The main route crosses six time zones and somewhere between five and a half and six days of non-stop driving, which no sane person attempts non-stop, because somewhere in northern Ontario the moose will have an opinion about your schedule.

Every Province, Not a Single Territory

Here's the fact that catches people. The Trans-Canada Highway touches all ten provinces: British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, Québec, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland and Labrador. It reaches Prince Edward Island via the Confederation Bridge, a 13-kilometre span that opened in 1997 and finally connected the island to the rest of the country by road.

What it does not touch is any of the three territories: Yukon, Northwest Territories, or Nunavut. For a country whose identity is so bound up with the North, the polar bear on our toonie, the Inukshuk on our Olympic mittens, the very idea of "True North strong and free," the great national highway pointedly does not go north. It runs along the southern belly of the country, hugging the U.S. border for much of its length, because that is where the people are. The North remains, as it has for most of Canadian history, somewhere you get to by plane.

There's a geographical reason for this, and it's the same reason most Canadians live within a couple hundred kilometres of the American border: the Canadian Shield. That vast expanse of ancient rock, lakes, and thin soil that covers half the country is magnificent and nearly useless for driving through. The highway bends around its edges rather than punching through its heart, because punching through the Shield is an exercise in expensive futility that generations of engineers have declined to attempt.

The Ferry That Finishes the Job

And then there's the bit that really confuses people. The eastern terminus of the Trans-Canada Highway is in St. John's, Newfoundland, an island. To get your car there from the mainland, you cannot simply drive. You must board the Marine Atlantic ferry from North Sydney, Nova Scotia, for a crossing of roughly six to eight hours (longer in rough weather, of which the North Atlantic provides a generous and unrelenting supply).

This is not a quirk or an oversight. It's the honest answer to a geographical problem: Newfoundland is an island, and islands, by definition, are not connected to the mainland by road. The ferry is the road. The Trans-Canada Highway simply continues, in spirit and in signage, across a stretch of open ocean, and you complete those kilometres not behind the wheel but on the deck of a ship, drinking a bad coffee and watching for whales.

It means that, strictly speaking, nobody has ever driven the entire Trans-Canada Highway. They've driven most of it and ferried the rest. The claim "I drove across Canada" always contains, for the purists, a small asterisk. The asterisk is a ferry.

A Road Built in a Hurry

The highway we know is younger than you might guess for something that feels so permanent. Construction was authorized in 1949 and the route officially opened in 1962, though stretches were paved and upgraded for decades after. The impetus was partly national pride, every modern country wanted a coast-to-coast road, and partly the dawning realization that a country joined only by rail was a country whose cities couldn't quite see each other.

Even now, calling it "paved" is generous in places. Long stretches through northern Ontario and the prairies are a single lane each way, and for years the route through British Columbia was a white-knuckle affair of narrow mountain passes. The road has been straightened, widened, and tamed gradually, the way Canada does most things: slowly, over a long distance, with a certain resignation about the weather.

Final Thoughts

The Trans-Canada Highway is not the biggest road, or the fastest, or the most scenic, though it is occasionally all three. It's the road that tries, across mountains and Shield and prairie and ferry crossing, to hold a country together that is, by any reasonable measure, too big and too empty to hold together at all. That it manages this by hugging the southern edge and asking you to take a boat at the end is, in its way, perfectly Canadian. We built the road where the people are, and we accepted, as we accept most things, that the rest would have to wait.

So if you ever set out to drive the whole thing: budget a week, watch for moose, and when you reach Nova Scotia, drive your car onto a ferry and stand on the deck. You're not cheating. You're finishing the road the only way it can be finished, the way it was always meant to be.

Keep the rubber side down, my friends.

P.S. The official Trans-Canada route marker, that distinctive green-and-white maple-leaf sign, is one of the few things in Canada that looks exactly the same in every province. The road underneath it, however, very much does not. That's probably the most honest thing about the whole enterprise.
